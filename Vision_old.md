# Core Vision

A living space simulation where the player exists inside a large universe that continues changing independently of them.

The player is not the center of the galaxy.

The universe contains:

* factions
* politics
* trade
* economies
* conflict
* infrastructure
* people
* changing information
* long-term events

The player begins small and grows into a larger role inside that universe without ever completely controlling it.

The game is primarily a sandbox.

The universe should feel alive because things are actually happening.

The economy is a primary driving force behind the universe.

Resources, manufacturing, logistics, shortages, and faction dependencies influence political stability, trade, conflict, and opportunity.

The universe continues functioning even without player involvement.

NPC factions, industries, traders, pirates, patrols, and civilian operations remain active independently of players.

---

# Universe View

The main screen is primarily a **galaxy / universe view**.

The player sees many solar systems spread across space.

The player can:

* select systems
* zoom into systems
* move between map layers

The universe uses layered simulation depth.

Systems with player presence, trusted crew presence, owned assets, or meaningful visibility may operate in higher detail.

In higher-detail simulation, NPC ships physically perform operations.

Examples:

* hauling cargo
* mining resources
* trading goods
* patrol activity
* piracy
* industrial work
* military movement

Markets replenish because goods were actually transported.

Systems outside player visibility continue running through lower-cost abstract simulation.

The universe remains alive everywhere, but simulation detail scales based on relevance, visibility, and performance requirements.

When awareness returns, systems reconcile into updated concrete state.

# Fog of War / Information Philosophy

The player does not possess perfect information.

Information depends on presence, assets, crew, and intelligence.

If the player or trusted crew are present in a system:

high-detail awareness is available.

If the player lacks presence:

information becomes limited or outdated.

Static knowledge may remain.

Dynamic reality may not.

Examples:

The player may know a planet exists.

The player may not know:

* faction ownership changed
* a station was destroyed
* pirate activity increased
* trade collapsed
* military occupation occurred

Player knowledge and actual reality are separate concepts.

---

# Living Universe Simulation

The universe continues changing in real time.

However, not every location requires full high-detail simulation all the time.

Systems with player presence, crew presence, or active assets may run in higher detail.

Other systems may run through more abstract background simulation.

Systems can catch up when player awareness returns.

The player should feel:

*"things happened while I was away."*

# Factions and Galaxy Structure

The galaxy contains many solar systems.

Major factions possess home systems.

Faction home systems are separated by buffer regions. Buffers must be at least 3 jumps. 

To travel to another faction player must pass though at least one lawless zone. 

Home systems tend to be safer.

Outer systems become increasingly uncertain and dangerous.

Reputation matters.

Faction territory safety depends partly on how factions view the player.

The universe contains ongoing political activity, conflict, and large-scale events.

The project uses original factions and lore.

Inspirational influences are not copied directly.

There should be at least five factions

One major hidden faction currently conceptualized as "The Dissonance."

A covert manipulative power operating through destabilization, indirect influence, ideology, hidden agendas, and long-term strategic objectives rather than conventional open military dominance.

Their true nature, structure, motives, and origins remain intentionally uncertain.

Earth — dominant human authority

Mars — underground insurgent / separatist movement seeking support

Earth is not intended to be a purely heroic or villainous civilization.

The dominant human authority is a large, complex, politically layered power balancing security, economics, diplomacy, and internal stability.

Different political interests exist inside Earth.

Some groups favor stability, reform, diplomacy, and institutional continuity.

Others favor stronger surveillance, tighter security policy, harsher anti-insurgency responses, and more authoritarian approaches.

Mars is not a conventional peer-state military rival.

The Mars movement operates more as an underground insurgency, separatist movement, covert network, and political resistance effort seeking legitimacy, resources, allies, and external support.

Earth remains significantly stronger than Mars in conventional power projection.

Mars survival may depend partly on covert assistance, foreign sympathizers, hidden infrastructure, smuggling, intelligence networks, or relationships with powers dissatisfied with Earth.

Alien factions — competing major civilizations

The Dissonance — hidden destabilizing influence operating beneath visible politics

Major factions are not intended to represent simplistic one-species archetypes.

Factions function more as civilizations, political blocs, cultural spheres, coalitions, or major power structures.

Multiple species, minority populations, aligned groups, corporations, and competing internal interests may exist inside major faction space.

Faction identity should emerge from history, politics, economics, ideology, culture, and strategic behavior rather than purely biological differences.

Major faction home regions are intentionally separated by significant distance, buffer space, and dangerous transit corridors.

Inter-faction dependency is expected.

Critical goods, technologies, medicines, resources, and industrial materials may require long-distance transportation across unstable, lawless, contested, or pirate-heavy regions.

Distance is intended to create meaningful logistical pressure.

Lawless space, piracy, convoy protection, escort operations, diplomacy, smuggling, and strategic transport should matter because critical trade and survival may depend on them.

Travel routes are not merely movement paths.

They are economic, political, and strategic lifelines.

# Mission Philosophy

Mission content should primarily emerge from living simulation state.

The universe generates needs, opportunities, crises, shortages, conflicts, and operational problems.

Players interact with those conditions.

Examples:

* pirate suppression because trade routes are disrupted
* convoy escort operations
* emergency cargo shortages
* industrial repair contracts
* faction security responses
* recovery operations
* exploration opportunities
* intelligence or smuggling work

The game may include limited authored story content, tutorials, onboarding, and important narrative arcs.

However, systemic mission generation is a major design goal.

# Travel Philosophy

Interstellar movement relies heavily on a jump gate network.

Most ships depend on existing gates.

Advanced or extremely capable ships may eventually support independent jump capability.

Travel matters.

Distance matters.

Moving between safe major regions often requires multiple jumps through less secure territory.

# Economy Philosophy

The economy should not feel magical.

Goods should exist because someone mined, refined, manufactured, transported, traded, or consumed them.

Markets are connected to the living universe.

Cities, stations, colonies, and planets consume resources.

Production chains matter.

Trade routes matter.

Shortages matter.

Logistics matter.

NPC ships should generally have actual purposes.

Not decorative traffic.

Examples:

* mining
* hauling
* patrol
* escort
* industrial work
* trade operations

Mission opportunities can emerge naturally from real economic conditions.

The economy is not decorative.

Faction economies depend on actual resources, production capability, logistics, and access to critical goods.

Certain materials, technologies, or resources may primarily originate inside specific faction regions.

Dependencies create natural trade relationships, strategic vulnerabilities, shortages, black markets, political pressure, and potential conflict.

Disrupting transport, piracy, war, embargoes, or infrastructure damage can affect real economic conditions.

Mission opportunities may emerge from genuine simulation state rather than purely authored content.

Each economic location maintains real stored quantities of goods.

Markets are based on planets, stations, colonies, shipyards, mining sites, and other economic locations.

A location's market supply depends on its actual inventory, production, consumption, imports, and exports.

Goods can run out.

A market may have zero available units of a needed good.

Shortages create demand, raise prices, trigger NPC logistics behavior, and generate player opportunities.

Locations may request goods through NPC trade, faction supply chains, emergency contracts, smuggling, or player missions.

Example:

Mars may have zero food available and high food demand.

Earth may produce excess food and sell it cheaply.

A player or NPC trader can buy food from Earth, transport it to Mars, and sell it for profit.

If pirates, embargoes, war, or infrastructure failure prevent delivery, the shortage can continue or worsen.

The economy is intended to act as a primary driver of larger universe behavior.

Economic conditions influence diplomacy, politics, security policy, shortages, conflict, trade agreements, smuggling, faction strategy, and long-term events.

Critical goods, technologies, medicines, industrial components, food supplies, fuels, or strategic materials may exist unevenly across faction space.

Dependency creates leverage.

Shortages create pressure.

A faction facing crisis may respond through trade negotiations, diplomacy, emergency logistics, black markets, covert operations, technological substitution, political compromise, military action, or acceptance of loss.

The economy is not isolated from politics or conflict.

It helps drive them.

Players interact with these pressures through commerce, missions, logistics, intelligence work, smuggling, industrial operations, humanitarian support, military contracting, and independent decision-making.

# Player Philosophy

The player begins as an ordinary person with limited resources.

No chosen hero.

No galaxy ruler.

The player starts with only enough money to begin space life.

Possibly through:

* inheritance
* savings
* starting funds

The player chooses a starter ship.

The player gradually builds:

* money
* experience
* crew
* ships
* influence
* infrastructure

The player remains a real physical person inside the universe.

No magical teleportation between owned assets.

---

The project leans more toward strategic management, operational leadership, simulation, and hybrid control rather than traditional first-person shooter gameplay.

Direct participation may exist.

However, long-term gameplay increasingly emphasizes delegation, crew leadership, logistics, ship coordination, economic operations, and organizational decision-making.

# Player Character

The player character uses character creation.

Backstories influence starting identity.

Backstories affect:

* skills
* traits
* strengths
* drawbacks

Tradeoffs matter.

The player can personally participate in ship operations while still acting as leader and commander.

# Player Control Philosophy

The game uses a hybrid control model.

Players may personally operate their own ship when desired.

Players may also issue higher-level operational commands.

Crew management and delegation are important long-term gameplay elements.

As operations grow, players increasingly coordinate ships, crews, logistics, assignments, and strategic decisions.

The intended fantasy blends personal participation with organizational leadership.

The player is neither purely a direct-action pilot nor a completely detached strategy controller.

# Ground and Planet Operations

The universe may include planetary, station, surface, or localized operational missions.

Ground gameplay is not intended to be a traditional first-person shooter system.

Operations lean more toward simulation, crew management, tactical choices, skill checks, equipment, and operational outcomes.

Crew quality, training, equipment, traits, and player preparation influence results.

Possible presentation styles may include:

* management interfaces
* event-driven encounters
* tactical mission views
* lightweight visual simulation
* simplified operational representations

Players may personally participate in some operations without requiring full FPS gameplay scope.

* flexible slots

Experience matters.

# Long-Term Progression

The game is a sandbox without a traditional ending.

Late progression may include:

* multiple ships
* trusted crews
* remote operations
* small infrastructure
* player-owned station(s)
* modest prosperity
* meaningful regional influence

The intended fantasy is successful independence and operational growth.

Not galactic domination.

"Penthouse with a view, not owner of the entire building."

The intended long-term fantasy is becoming a powerful independent operator, fleet owner, or corporate-scale organization operating within larger faction power structures.

Players gain influence, wealth, ships, crews, and operational capability.

Players do not become dominant galactic rulers or fully sovereign major factions.

# Example: Interconnected Systems Philosophy

The project's systems are intended to influence one another rather than operate in isolation.

Example scenario:

The player is operating in a distant system and lacks direct visibility into Sol due to fog of war and information limitations.

Through available news feeds, intelligence reports, or delayed information sources, the player learns of growing tensions between Earth and Mars.

Additional reports suggest worsening food shortages and civilian hardship on Mars.

The player chooses to investigate.

Upon arriving, the player discovers a deeper situation.

Earth is pressuring the public Martian government to surrender underground rebel elements.

Food shipments have been reduced or restricted as leverage.

Mars is attempting to resist political pressure while dealing with worsening shortages.

This situation affects multiple core systems simultaneously.

Economy:

Mars markets may show severe food shortages, depleted inventory, rising prices, black markets, or unavailable goods.

Trade:

Food may need to be acquired from distant regions outside Earth influence.

Long-distance logistics, travel risk, and supply chain complexity become meaningful.

Politics:

Earth policy, insurgency pressure, diplomacy, faction relationships, sanctions, and political conflict drive the crisis.

News and Information:

The player initially learns of events through imperfect information systems rather than omniscient awareness.

Different news sources, intelligence quality, propaganda, or incomplete reporting may shape player understanding.

Missions:

Systemic mission opportunities emerge naturally.

Examples:

* smuggle food into Mars
* escort supply convoys
* obtain supplies from remote regions
* support legal trade
* operate through black markets
* intelligence gathering
* covert transport

Travel and Risk:

Obtaining supplies may require long-distance travel across lawless space, unstable regions, hostile territory, or politically sensitive routes.

Skills and Crew:

Pilot skill, stealth capability, crew quality, navigation, smuggling ability, operational planning, or diplomatic choices may influence outcomes.

Security and Enforcement:

Earth patrols, inspections, interdiction, surveillance, or military presence may create operational danger.

Player Choice:

The player may profit from shortages, provide humanitarian aid, support Mars, cooperate with Earth, exploit black markets, remain neutral, or pursue entirely independent goals.

The intended design philosophy is that economy, politics, news, travel, missions, skills, logistics, factions, and player decisions should naturally interact rather than exist as disconnected systems.
